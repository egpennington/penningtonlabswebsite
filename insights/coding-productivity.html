Coding Productivity · Part 1

Speed isn't the whole story.

What happens when we can create software faster than we can understand it?

Why I created this model

In 2025, I attended an ION meetup in Houston. My reason for going was simple: I wanted to meet other programmers.

I'd already spent years teaching myself to code, worked through Scrimba's boot camp, and even placed second at the Galveston Hackathon several years earlier. Programming had become an important part of my life, and I wanted to spend time around people who shared that interest.

The evening's workshop happened to be about AI-assisted development, commonly called vibe coding. It was presented by William Fermo, a cofounder of a running company started by three brothers and, at the time, Vice President of Code4Y'all.

But the subject wasn't what brought me there. I would have attended a workshop titled "How to Print Hello World" if it meant getting to hang out with other coders.

What I hadn't expected was how much the workshop would make me think.

For years, I'd invested time and effort into learning to program. I enjoyed the challenge of figuring things out, understanding how code worked, and building something myself.

Now AI was offering a different approach. Describe what you want, and let the machine help build it.

That was intriguing. But it also raised a question I couldn't easily dismiss.

Did I want to learn AI-assisted development, or did I want to continue learning to write code myself?

Was using AI going to accelerate my development as a programmer, or would it allow me to produce applications without developing the understanding I'd worked so hard to acquire?

After the meetup, I sat down with a piece of paper and began working through that dilemma.

I remembered Michael Porter's work on competitive strategy, particularly his concept of being stuck in the middle. Porter examined how businesses could struggle when they failed to establish a clear competitive strategy.

His work wasn't about programming or artificial intelligence, but the underlying tension between competing approaches stayed with me.

I began thinking about my own dilemma in terms of two variables: speed and understanding.

What if I could build applications quickly but didn't understand the code? What if I understood everything I wrote but spent so much time perfecting it that I rarely finished anything? And was it possible to combine the speed of AI-assisted development with the deeper understanding that comes from learning to program?

I drew two axes on the paper.

One represented speed. The other represented understanding.

Four quadrants emerged: Stuck, Perfection Trap, Automation Trap, and Flow.

That sketch became what I call the Coding Productivity Model.

I didn't create it to tell programmers whether they should or shouldn't use AI. I created it to help myself understand a choice I was facing.

And I eventually realized that perhaps the question wasn't whether to learn AI or continue learning to code.

Perhaps the real challenge was learning how to do both without sacrificing either.

Four places a developer can find themselves

The aim isn't to judge developers by a quadrant. It's to notice where our habits are taking us. Slow, careful work can be valuable. Fast AI-assisted work can be valuable too. The danger comes when we mistake output for mastery.

Writing more code isn't the same thing as making more progress.

At Pennington Labs, this is a question worth returning to as we build: how can we use powerful tools to move faster while still growing our ability to reason, test, and make good decisions?

This is the starting point. The next question is how we move toward Flow intentionally—without trading understanding for speed.
