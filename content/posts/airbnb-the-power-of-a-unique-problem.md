## Three Air Mattresses and a Different Question

In 2007, Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia had a problem. They couldn't afford the rent on their San Francisco apartment.

Meanwhile, a major design conference was coming to town, and hotel rooms were scarce.

Their solution? Three air mattresses, breakfast, and a simple website called *AirBed & Breakfast*.

Three guests stayed in their apartment. An experiment born out of necessity would eventually become Airbnb.

## The Uniqueness Principle

In *Breakthrough Thinking*, Gerald Nadler and Shozo Hibino introduce the **Uniqueness Principle**: every problem has its own circumstances, people, and relationships. We shouldn't assume an existing solution will fit simply because two problems look similar.

Airbnb's founders offer an interesting example.

They weren't initially asking how to build another hotel chain. They recognized an unusual combination of circumstances: people needed affordable lodging, and they had unused space.

Their solution grew from those specific conditions.

Of course, this doesn't mean the founders deliberately followed Breakthrough Thinking. But their story demonstrates why examining what makes a situation different can reveal possibilities that conventional solutions overlook.

## The Question for Developers

When building software, how often do we immediately reach for an existing framework, template, or familiar solution?

Perhaps the first question shouldn't be:

**"How has someone else solved this?"**

But instead:

**"What makes this particular problem unique?"**

Sometimes the breakthrough isn't a better version of an existing solution.

Sometimes it's recognizing that you're solving a different problem altogether.

Uniqueness isn't necessarily about inventing something nobody has ever imagined. It's about understanding what makes the situation in front of you different.
